How does a classical bit differ from a qubit superposition?

A deterministic or probabilistic classical bit is fundamentally limited to representing definite states or classical mixtures of probabilities, whereas a qubit in a coherent superposition exists in a linear combination of basis states governed by complex probability amplitudes. This distinction enables quantum systems to exhibit phase relationships and interference effects that classical bits cannot replicate.

Deterministic and Probabilistic Classical Bits

In classical computing, a deterministic bit is the simplest unit of information. At any given moment, it occupies one of two mutually exclusive states, denoted as \(0\) or \(1\).

A probabilistic classical bit extends this model by introducing uncertainty. Instead of being strictly \(0\) or \(1\), the bit is described by a probability distribution over the two states:

\[P(0) = p, \quad P(1) = 1 - p\]

where \(p\) is a real number satisfying \(0 \le p \le 1\). Crucially, a probabilistic bit is always in one definite underlying state—either \(0\) or \(1\)—and the probabilities simply reflect our incomplete knowledge of which state it is in. When operations are applied to a probabilistic bit, probabilities combine linearly according to classical probability theory, meaning they can only add together and never cancel each other out.

Coherent Superposition in a Qubit

A qubit, or quantum bit, operates according to the principles of quantum mechanics. Rather than being restricted to \(0\) or \(1\), a qubit can exist in a coherent superposition of its computational basis states, \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). Its state vector \(|\psi\rangle\) is written as:

\[|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\]

Here, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are complex numbers known as probability amplitudes. To ensure the total probability of all measurement outcomes equals \(1\), these amplitudes must satisfy the normalization condition:

\[|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\]

When a qubit is measured in the computational basis, it collapses to \(|0\rangle\) with probability \(|\alpha|^2\) and to \(|1\rangle\) with probability \(|\beta|^2\). However, prior to measurement, the qubit is not secretly in either \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\); it genuinely occupies the combined state \(|\psi\rangle\).

Complex Amplitudes and Quantum Interference

The most profound difference between a probabilistic bit and a qubit lies in the nature of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Because they are complex numbers, each amplitude has both a magnitude and a phase. While the global phase of \(|\psi\rangle\) has no observable effect, the relative phase between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) is physically significant.

When quantum gates manipulate a qubit, they transform the complex amplitudes directly. Because amplitudes can be positive, negative, or complex, different computational paths leading to the same outcome can interfere with one another:

  • Constructive interference occurs when amplitudes align in phase, increasing the probability of measuring a specific state.
  • Destructive interference occurs when amplitudes are out of phase, canceling each other out and reducing the probability of an outcome to zero.

A probabilistic classical bit cannot experience interference because classical probabilities are strictly non-negative real numbers.

Geometric Representation

Geometrically, the state space of a probabilistic bit is a one-dimensional line segment connecting \(0\) and \(1\), parameterized by the single real number \(p\). In contrast, the pure state space of a single qubit is represented by the surface of a three-dimensional unit sphere known as the Bloch sphere. Any point on this surface corresponds to a valid coherent superposition, capturing both the probability weights and the continuous relative phase between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\).
