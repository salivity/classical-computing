How Does Electromigration Displace Metal Atoms in Wires?

Electromigration is the gradual movement of metal atoms in a conductor caused by the momentum transfer from flowing electrons. In classical computing, as integrated circuits scale down and interconnect wires become narrower, high current densities accelerate this atomic displacement. Over time, the migration of atoms creates voids that break electrical connections and hillocks that cause short circuits, leading to chip failure.

The Physics of Momentum Transfer

When an electric current flows through a metal interconnect, such as copper or aluminum, conduction electrons move from the negative terminal (cathode) toward the positive terminal (anode). As these electrons travel through the crystal lattice of the metal, they collide with thermally vibrating metal ions.

Two competing forces act on the metal ions during this process:

  • The Direct Electrostatic Force (\(F_{\text{direct}}\)): The electric field pulls the positively charged metal ions toward the cathode.
  • The Electron Wind Force (\(F_{\text{wind}}\)): The stream of electrons transfers momentum to the metal ions, pushing them toward the anode.

In most metals used in classical computing, the electron wind force is significantly stronger than the direct electrostatic force. The net force \(F_{\text{net}}\) acting on an ion can be expressed as:

\[F_{\text{net}} = (Z_{\text{el}} + Z_{\text{wind}}) e E = Z^* e E\]

Here, \(e\) is the elementary charge, \(E\) is the electric field, and \(Z^*\) is the effective valence, which is typically negative because the electron wind dominates.

Why Narrow Interconnects Are Vulnerable

As transistor sizes shrink, the cross-sectional area \(A\) of the metal wires connecting them decreases. For a given current \(I\), the current density \(J\) increases inversely with the area:

\[J = \frac{I}{A}\]

When current density reaches high levels—often exceeding \(10^5\text{ A/cm}^2\) in modern processors—the rate of electron-ion collisions rises dramatically. Additionally, narrow wires have a higher surface-to-volume ratio. Atoms located at grain boundaries, interfaces, and surfaces are less tightly bound than those inside the bulk crystal lattice, making them much easier to dislodge.

The Role of Diffusion Pathways

Metal atoms do not move uniformly through the wire; instead, they follow paths of least resistance:

  1. Grain Boundary Diffusion: Polycrystalline metals consist of many small crystal grains. The boundaries between these grains have irregular atomic arrangements and lower activation energies, serving as fast highways for atomic movement.
  2. Interface Diffusion: In modern copper interconnects, atoms often migrate along the interface between the copper and the surrounding barrier or capping layers.
  3. Surface Diffusion: Atoms on the outer edges of the wire can move more freely due to fewer neighboring bonds.

Void and Hillock Formation

Because atomic flux is not uniform throughout an interconnect, regions of flux divergence appear. Where more atoms leave a region than enter it, vacancies accumulate and eventually coalesce into a void. A growing void reduces the wire's cross-sectional area, further increasing the local current density and accelerating heating until an open circuit occurs.

Conversely, where more atoms arrive than leave, material piles up to form a hillock or whisker. These protrusions can crack the surrounding insulating dielectric and touch adjacent wires, creating an unintended short circuit.

Predicting Mean Time to Failure

Engineers estimate the lifespan of interconnects under electromigration using Black's equation, which relates the Mean Time to Failure (\(\text{MTTF}\)) to current density and temperature:

\[\text{MTTF} = A J^{-n} \exp\left(\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right)\]

In this equation, \(A\) is a constant based on wire geometry and material, \(n\) is a scaling factor (typically between \(1\) and \(2\)), \(E_a\) is the activation energy for diffusion, \(k_B\) is the Boltzmann constant, and \(T\) is the absolute temperature. Because temperature appears in the exponent, Joule heating from high current densities compounds the problem, exponentially shortening the wire's lifetime.
