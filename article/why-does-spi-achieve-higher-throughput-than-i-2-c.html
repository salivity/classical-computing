Why Does SPI Achieve Higher Throughput Than I2C?

The Serial Peripheral Interface achieves higher throughput than the Inter-Integrated Circuit protocol because it uses separate unidirectional data lines for simultaneous full-duplex communication, dedicated chip-select pins that eliminate addressing overhead, and push-pull drivers that support much faster clock speeds. While I2C prioritizes pin efficiency and multi-device simplicity on a shared two-wire bus, SPI trades extra physical pins for raw data transfer speed and lower protocol overhead in classical computing systems.

Full-Duplex Data Lines vs. Half-Duplex Sharing

SPI uses two separate data lines: Master Out Slave In (MOSI) and Master In Slave Out (MISO). Because each line carries data in only one direction, the controller and the peripheral can transmit and receive bits simultaneously on every clock cycle. This full-duplex architecture doubles the effective bandwidth compared to a half-duplex bus when bidirectional data exchange is needed.

In contrast, I2C relies on a single bidirectional data line (SDA) for both transmitting and receiving. Only one device can drive the SDA line at any given time, which forces communication into half-duplex mode. If a controller needs to send a command and read back sensor data, it must first complete the write phase, release or reverse the bus direction, and then clock in the response.

Chip-Select Pins vs. Protocol Addressing Overhead

SPI uses dedicated hardware chip-select (CS) or slave-select (SS) pins to choose which peripheral is active. Pulling a specific peripheral's CS line low immediately prepares that device for communication without sending any address bits over the data lines. Once selected, every clock pulse transfers useful payload data.

I2C avoids extra select pins by embedding a 7-bit or 10-bit device address into the data stream at the start of every transaction. In addition to the address bits, I2C requires a read/write bit, start and stop conditions, and an acknowledgment (ACK/NACK) bit after every byte transferred. For a standard 8-bit byte in I2C, at least 9 clock cycles are needed, meaning protocol overhead consumes at least \(\frac{1}{9} \approx 11.1\%\) of the clock cycles just for acknowledgments, plus the initial addressing frame.

Push-Pull Drivers vs. Open-Drain Pull-Up Resistors

The electrical design of the bus also limits how fast each protocol can be clocked. SPI outputs use push-pull drivers, which actively drive the signal lines both high and low. Active driving produces sharp, fast rising and falling edges, allowing SPI clocks to routinely operate at tens of megahertz—often \(10\text{ MHz}\) to \(50\text{ MHz}\) or higher depending on board layout.

I2C uses open-drain (or open-collector) drivers on both SDA and SCL. Devices can only pull the lines low; returning a line to a high state depends on external pull-up resistors charging the bus capacitance. The RC time constant \(\tau = R \cdot C\) slows down the rising edge, which restricts standard I2C speeds to \(100\text{ kHz}\), Fast Mode to \(400\text{ kHz}\), and Fast Mode Plus to \(1\text{ MHz}\). Even specialized High-Speed I2C (\(3.4\text{ MHz}\)) remains significantly slower than typical SPI implementations.

Summary of Trade-Offs

SPI achieves superior throughput by dedicating more pins to the interface—typically four wires (SCLK, MOSI, MISO, CS) plus an additional CS line per peripheral. This hardware investment removes bus arbitration, addressing frames, acknowledgment bits, and slow open-drain rise times, making SPI the preferred choice for high-speed peripherals such as flash memory, displays, and high-rate analog-to-digital converters.
