How Do GAA Nanosheets Improve Electrostatic Control?

Gate-all-around nanosheet transistors improve electrostatic control at extreme process nodes by wrapping the gate material completely around horizontally stacked silicon channels. This four-sided gate architecture eliminates the un-gated bottom leakage path found in FinFETs, allowing the gate to modulate channel potential more uniformly and suppress short-channel effects as gate lengths shrink below 3 nanometers.

Why FinFETs Struggle at Extreme Nodes

As transistor dimensions scale down into the sub-3-nanometer regime, the physical distance between the source and drain—known as the gate length \(L_g\)—becomes so short that the drain's electric field begins to compete with the gate for control over the channel. This phenomenon, called Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL), makes it harder to turn the transistor completely off and increases subthreshold leakage current.

In a FinFET, the gate wraps around three sides of a vertical silicon fin (top, left, and right). However, the bottom of the fin remains connected to the substrate, leaving an un-gated path where electric field lines from the drain can penetrate. To maintain control, FinFETs require taller, thinner fins, which eventually hit mechanical stability limits and suffer from increased quantum confinement and surface scattering.

The Gate-All-Around (GAA) Advantage

A gate-all-around nanosheet transistor replaces the vertical fin with a stack of thin, horizontal silicon sheets (nanosheets) suspended one above another. Because the high-\(\kappa\) metal gate completely surrounds all four sides of each nanosheet, electrostatic coupling between the gate and the channel is maximized.

The effectiveness of electrostatic control is often characterized by the natural scaling length \(\lambda\), which represents how far the drain's electric field penetrates into the channel. For a FinFET (tri-gate) versus a GAA nanosheet, \(\lambda\) is significantly smaller in the GAA structure because the gate surrounds the entire perimeter of the channel. A smaller \(\lambda\) allows for a shorter gate length \(L_g\) without triggering severe short-channel effects, following the general scaling rule:

\[L_g \gtrsim 2.5 \lambda\]

By reducing \(\lambda\), GAA nanosheets enable steeper subthreshold slopes—approaching the theoretical room-temperature limit of \(60\text{ mV/decade}\)—which means the transistor switches between its on and off states more abruptly with less wasted power.

Variable Channel Width and Drive Current

Beyond electrostatics, nanosheets offer a key layout advantage over FinFETs. In FinFET technology, drive current is quantized by the number of discrete fins; you cannot easily make a fin half as wide to tune power versus performance.

With GAA nanosheets, the effective channel width \(W_{\text{eff}}\) is determined by the perimeter of each sheet multiplied by the number of stacked sheets \(N\):

\[W_{\text{eff}} = 2N(W_{\text{sheet}} + T_{\text{sheet}})\]

where \(W_{\text{sheet}}\) is the nanosheet width and \(T_{\text{sheet}}\) is its thickness. Designers can continuously vary \(W_{\text{sheet}}\) during lithography to optimize individual transistors—using wider sheets for high-performance logic paths and narrower sheets for low-power circuits—while keeping the vertical footprint compact.

Overcoming Parasitic Challenges

While wrapping the gate around all four sides improves electrostatics, it also introduces new parasitic capacitance between the gate and the source/drain regions. To mitigate this, advanced GAA processes incorporate inner spacers made of low-\(\kappa\) dielectric materials between the stacked nanosheets. These spacers isolate the gate metal from the source/drain epitaxy, preserving the switching speed gains achieved through superior electrostatic control.
