Why Does DRAM Use Only One Transistor and One Capacitor?

Dynamic Random-Access Memory, or DRAM, stores each bit of data using just one transistor and one capacitor because this minimalist design maximizes memory density and lowers manufacturing cost per bit. By representing a binary 1 or 0 as the presence or absence of electrical charge on a tiny capacitor and using a single transistor as an access switch, DRAM achieves the compact footprint needed for gigabytes of main memory in classical computers.

The Role of the Capacitor and Transistor

In a 1T1C DRAM cell, the two components have distinct, complementary jobs:

  • The Capacitor: Acts as the storage element. It holds a small amount of electrical charge \(Q = C \cdot V\), where \(C\) is capacitance and \(V\) is voltage. A charged capacitor typically represents a logic 1, while a discharged capacitor represents a logic 0.
  • The Transistor: Acts as a gatekeeper (an access switch). Its gate is connected to a word line, and one of its terminals is connected to a bit line. When the word line is activated, the transistor turns on and connects the capacitor to the bit line so the stored charge can be read or written.

Because a capacitor is a passive component and a single MOSFET takes up very little silicon area, the entire cell is remarkably small compared to other memory technologies.

Why Not Use More Transistors Like SRAM?

Static RAM (SRAM), which is used in CPU caches, typically requires six transistors per bit cell (6T). Four transistors form cross-coupled inverters that actively hold the bit state as long as power is supplied, and two more act as access transistors.

While SRAM is much faster and does not need to be refreshed, six transistors consume roughly four to six times more silicon area than a 1T1C DRAM cell. For main memory—where capacities of 8 GB, 16 GB, or more are standard—using 6T cells would make memory chips prohibitively large, expensive, and power-hungry. DRAM trades away SRAM's speed and static retention in exchange for massive density.

The Trade-Offs of the 1T1C Design

Using only one transistor and one capacitor introduces two key physical challenges that DRAM controllers must manage:

  1. Charge Leakage and Refreshing: Capacitors are not perfect insulators; charge gradually leaks away through the transistor and dielectric material. To prevent data loss, DRAM must be periodically refreshed—read and rewritten every few milliseconds (typically every \(64\text{ ms}\)).
  2. Destructive Reads: Reading a DRAM cell connects the tiny cell capacitor to the much larger capacitance of the bit line, sharing the charge and altering the voltage on the cell. Because this process drains the cell's original charge, a sense amplifier must immediately amplify the small voltage change \(\Delta V\) and write the full charge back into the capacitor after every read.

Summary

DRAM relies on a single transistor and a single capacitor per bit because it is the simplest possible circuit capable of storing and isolating a binary state in silicon. Although this design requires constant refreshing and complex sense-amplification circuitry at the edges of the memory array, the dramatic savings in area per bit make it the ideal architecture for high-capacity main memory in classical computing.
