What Does Landauer's Principle State About Erasing One Bit?

Landauer's principle establishes a fundamental physical limit in classical computing by stating that erasing one bit of information inevitably dissipates a minimum amount of thermodynamic heat into the environment. This minimum energy cost is proportional to the temperature of the system and arises from the connection between information entropy and thermodynamic entropy. Understanding this principle explains why irreversible computation generates heat and sets a lower bound on the energy efficiency of modern computers.

The Core Statement of Landauer's Principle

Formulated by Rolf Landauer in 1961, the principle addresses logically irreversible operations—processes where the input cannot be uniquely reconstructed from the output. Erasing a bit (for example, resetting a memory cell to \(0\) regardless of whether its prior state was \(0\) or \(1\)) is a classic irreversible operation.

Landauer's principle states that erasing one bit of information in a system at temperature \(T\) requires the dissipation of at least:

\[Q \ge k_B T \ln 2\]

where:

  • \(Q\) is the heat dissipated into the thermal reservoir (environment).
  • \(k_B\) is the Boltzmann constant, approximately \(1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}\).
  • \(T\) is the absolute temperature of the environment in Kelvin.
  • \(\ln 2 \approx 0.6931\) is the natural logarithm of \(2\).

Why Information Erasure Generates Heat

The physical reason behind this limit lies in the second law of thermodynamics and phase space volume. Before erasure, a single bit can occupy two possible states (\(0\) or \(1\)), each with equal probability \(p = 1/2\). The Shannon entropy of this bit is \(H = 1\ \text{bit}\), which corresponds to a thermodynamic entropy of:

\[S = k_B \ln 2\]

When the bit is reset to a single known state (such as \(0\)), the number of accessible microstates in the memory's phase space is reduced by half, decreasing the entropy of the information-bearing degrees of freedom by \(\Delta S_{\text{sys}} = -k_B \ln 2\).

Because the second law of thermodynamics requires that the total entropy of the universe (system plus environment) cannot decrease:

\[\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{env}} \ge 0\]

the environment must absorb at least \(\Delta S_{\text{env}} \ge k_B \ln 2\) of entropy. At constant temperature \(T\), this entropy increase corresponds to a minimum heat transfer of \(Q = T \Delta S_{\text{env}} \ge k_B T \ln 2\) into the surroundings.

Practical Significance in Classical Computing

At room temperature (\(T \approx 300\ \text{K}\)), the Landauer limit is:

\[k_B T \ln 2 \approx (1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K})(300\ \text{K})(0.6931) \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}\]

While this value is orders of magnitude smaller than the energy currently consumed per logic operation in silicon transistors, it represents a hard physical boundary. Reversible computing architectures—which avoid erasing information by using one-to-one logical mappings—can in theory bypass this limit, whereas any classical computer performing irreversible bit erasure must pay at least the \(k_B T \ln 2\) thermodynamic cost.
