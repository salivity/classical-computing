Why Do MLC and QLC NAND Flash Trade Endurance for Density?

Multi-level cell and quad-level cell NAND flash memory achieve higher storage density by packing multiple bits into a single memory cell, but this comes at the direct cost of reduced write endurance. Storing more bits per cell requires dividing a fixed voltage range into increasingly narrow intervals, which makes the cell far more sensitive to physical wear, charge leakage, and electrical interference.

How NAND Flash Stores Data

NAND flash memory stores data in floating-gate or charge-trap transistors. Each cell holds a specific amount of electrical charge, which determines its threshold voltage (\(V_{th}\))—the voltage required to turn the transistor on.

  • Single-Level Cell (SLC): Stores 1 bit per cell using \(2^1 = 2\) distinct voltage states (0 and 1).
  • Multi-Level Cell (MLC): Stores 2 bits per cell using \(2^2 = 4\) voltage states (00, 01, 10, 11).
  • Triple-Level Cell (TLC): Stores 3 bits per cell using \(2^3 = 8\) voltage states.
  • Quad-Level Cell (QLC): Stores 4 bits per cell using \(2^4 = 16\) voltage states.

Because the total operating voltage window of a NAND cell is physically limited, increasing the number of states from 2 to 4 or 16 shrinks the voltage margin separating adjacent states.

Oxide Wear and Charge Leakage

Writing and erasing data in NAND flash relies on Fowler-Nordheim tunneling, a quantum mechanical process where high voltage forces electrons through a thin insulating tunnel oxide layer. Every Program/Erase (P/E) cycle causes microscopic damage to this oxide layer, creating charge traps.

Over time, this degradation leads to two major issues:

  1. Charge Leakage: Damaged oxide allows trapped electrons to escape more easily, causing the cell's \(V_{th}\) to drift.
  2. Threshold Voltage Shift: Trapped charges in the oxide itself alter the electric field, shifting the voltage required to read the cell accurately.

In an SLC cell, a large voltage margin separates the two states, so minor \(V_{th}\) shifts do not cause bit errors. In a QLC cell, however, the margin between 16 states is extremely narrow. Even a small amount of charge leakage or voltage drift can push a cell's \(V_{th}\) into an adjacent state, resulting in data corruption.

Precision Programming and Stress

To place electrons into one of 16 tightly packed voltage windows, QLC flash cannot use a single strong voltage pulse. Instead, it uses Incremental Step Pulse Programming (ISPP), applying a series of smaller, precisely controlled voltage pulses followed by verification reads.

This process means QLC cells spend more time under high-voltage stress per write operation, and the controller must perform more frequent error-correction (ECC) and wear-leveling operations. As a result, typical P/E cycle endurance drops from roughly \(100,000\) cycles for SLC down to \(3,000\)–\(10,000\) cycles for MLC, and often around \(1,000\) cycles or fewer for QLC.

Summary of the Trade-off

Higher density in MLC and QLC NAND flash is achieved by subdividing a fixed physical voltage window into \(2^n\) states. Because the tunnel oxide wears out at a roughly similar rate per P/E cycle regardless of cell type, the narrower voltage margins of MLC and QLC reach their failure threshold much sooner than SLC, trading write endurance for lower cost per gigabyte.
